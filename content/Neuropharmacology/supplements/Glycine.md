## Overview
Glycine is one of the simplest and most versatile [[amino acid|amino acids]]. 

###### Structure
Glycine ($\ce{H_{2}N-CH_{2}-COOH}$) is the simplest amino acid with a [[hydrogen]] atom as its side chain. 
This simplicity makes it unique among the 20 standard amino acids, as it is the only one that is achiral. 

###### Classification
It is classified as a [[non-essential amino acid]], meaning the body can synthesize it, and it is readily avaiable from dietary proteins. 

###### Biosynthetic Precursor
Glycine serves as a precursor in the synthesis of several crucial biomolecules, such as:
- [[glutathione]]
- [[creatine]]
- [[porphyrins]]

## Neurobiology

###### Inhibitory Neurotransmission
In the [[spinal cord]] and [[brainstem]], glycine acts as an inhibitory [[neurotransmitter]].
When glycine binds to its glycine receptors, which are [[Ionotropic Receptors|ligand-gated ion channels]], it causes an influx of chloride ions into the [[neurons|neuron]], hyperpolarizing the [[cell membranes|cell membrane]], making it less likely to fire an [[action potential]], thereby dampening neuronal excitability. 

##### Modulation of Excitatory Neurotransmission
In higher brain regions, glycine plays a modulatory role by acting as a co-[[agonist]] at the [[NMDA receptor]].
For the NMDA receptor to open its ion channel, it requires the simultaneous binding of both [[glutamate]] and Glycine (or [[D-serine]])
Thus, Glycine availability can directly influence excitatory signaling. 
Altered Glycine signaling and NMDA receptor function have been implicated in various conditions, including [[5.4c Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders|schizophrenia]] and various forms of [[chronic pain]]

##### Transport
The synaptic concentration of glycine is tightly regulated by specific glycine transporters, which remove glycine from the [[synapse|synaptic cleft]] to terminate its action. 
This regulation ensures the proper balance between inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmission.
