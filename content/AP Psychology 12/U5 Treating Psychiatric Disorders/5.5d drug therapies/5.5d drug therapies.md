The most widely used [[biomedical therapy|biomedical treatments]] today are *drug therapies*
They interact with the [[1.2 Overview of the Nervous System|Nervous System]] to treat [[psychological disorder|disorders]]

Most drugs for [[5.4a Anxiety Disorders|anxiety]] and [[5.4b Depressive Disorders|depression]] are prescribed by:
- primary care providers
- psychiatrists
- (in some US states) psychologists

[[1.3b Drugs!|psychoactive substances]] may interact with specific [[neurotransmitter|neurotransmitters]] in the [[Central Nervous System]] to address possible biochemical causes of psychological disorders.

![[psychopharmacology|clean no-t]]

Since the 1950s, discoveries in [[psychopharmacology]] have revolutionized the treatment of people with severe disorders.

To evaluate any new drug's effectiveness, researchers need to know:
- how many people recover *without* treatment, and how quickly?
- Is the recovery due to the drug, or the *placebo effect*. When patients or mental health workers expect positive results, they may see what they expect, and their expectations can alter the treatment

To control for this, researchers give half the participants the real drug, and half a similar-appearing placebo.
Because *neither* the staff nor patients know who gets which, this is a *double-blind procedure*

>[!psych] antianxiety drugs (anxiolytics)
>drugs used to control [[5.4a Anxiety Disorders|anxiety]] and agitation

They [[depressants|depress]] [[Central Nervous System]] activity
Some have been used in combination with [[psychotherapy]] to:
- enhance [[exposure therapies|exposure therapy]]'s [[extinction]] of learned fears
- relieve the symptoms of [[posttraumatic stress disorder|PTSD]] and [[obsessive-compulsive disorder]]

Some critics fear that antianxiety drugs may reduce symptoms without resolving the underlying problems, especially when used as an ongoing treatment.

>[!psych] antidepressant drugs
>drugs used to treat depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, [[5.4a Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders|Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders]], and PTSD. (Several widely used antidepressant drugs are [[Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitor|SSRIs]])

SSRIs, like [[Fluoxetine]] (Prozac), and [[Sertraline]] (Zoloft) and [[Paroxetine]] (Paxil), work by blocking the [[reuptake]] of [[serotonin]].

Some older [[antidepressants]] work by blocking the reabsorption or breakdown of both [[norepinephrine]] and serotonin. 
They're effective, but these dual-action drugs have side effects, such as dry mouth, weight gain, hypertension and dizzy spells. Administering them using a patch, bypassing the intestines and liver, can help reduce some of these effects.

> [!hint] Textbook difference
> The textbook states that older antidepressants work by blocking the reabsorption or breakdown of both norepinephrine and serotonin. 
> They are obviously referring to [[tricyclic antidepressants|TCAs]], which block reabsorption, and [[Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors|MAOIs]], which block breakdown
> 
> The famously difficult side effect profile of TCAs does not really come from the fact that they inhibit both Serotonin and Norepinephrine; modern [[Serotonin-Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitor|SNRIs]] do exactly that. 
> Their effects are because they are even less selective, as they can interact with not just Serotonin and Norepinephrine transporters, but other receptors, like Histamine, [[acetylcholine]], and [[alpha-1 receptors|alpha-1 adrenergic receptors]]
> 
>MAOIs problems do not stem from side-effects at all, but rather dietary restrictions. 
>MAOIs inhibit the breakdown of neurotransmitters, but also of tyramine, so tyramine-rich foods must be avoided to avoid risk of tyramine-poisoning

SSRIs begin to influence neurotransmission within hours, but their full psychological effect may take *four weeks*. 
One possible reason is that increased serotonin promotes new [[synapse|synapses]], plus [[neurogenesis]], the birth of new [[neurons]] - possibly reversing [[stress]]-induced loss of neurons.

> [!hint] Textbook Difference
> Another just as significant factor, is that it takes time to desensitize [[serotonin autoreceptors]], especially the [[5-HT1A]] receptor. Until it is desensitized, activation of this receptor by serotonin will inhibit the production of further serotonin. This is why a new modern class of antidepressants, [[Serotonin Modulator and Stimulator|SMSs]], involve not only reuptake blocking, but also [[antagonist|antagonism]] or [[partial agonist|partial agonism]] of this receptor to desensitize it quicker.
