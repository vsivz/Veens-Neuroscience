>[!psych] hypnosis
> a social interaction in which one person (the hypnotist) suggests to another (the subject) that certain perceptions, feelings, thoughts or behaviors will spontaneously occur;
>in a [[psychotherapy|therapeutic]] context, the hypnotist attempts to use suggestion to reduce unpleasant physical sensations or emotions

###### mechanism
The hypnotist may invite you to:
- sit back
- fix gaze on a spot high on the wall
- relax
They may say things like:
- "Your eyes are growing tired"
- "Your eyelids are becoming heavy... now heavier"
- "They are beginning to close"
- "Your are becoming more relaxed"

After a few minutes of this *hypnotic induction*, one may experience *hypnosis*

###### Effects
Hypnosis has been shown to reduce pain, and make a persistent reduction in [[5.4a Anxiety Disorders|anxiety]]
Research has disproved many other claims however, including recovery of supposedly [[repression|repressed]] memories, or age-regression

In surgical experiments, hypnotized patients have required less medication, recovered sooner, and left the hospital earlier than unhypnotized patients in a control group

~10% of people can become so hypnotized major surgery can be performed without anesthetia
~50% can gain some pain relief from hypnosis


There are two main theories as to how hypnosis works:
###### Social influence Theory
This contends that hypnosis is a by-product of normal social and mental processes
Hypnotized people conform to their [[role]], behaving in ways appropriate for a good subject
This may allow the hypnotist to direct their attention away from pain

###### Dissociation theory
This proposes that hypnosis is a special dual-processing state of [[dissociation]] - a split between different levels of consciousness

![[dissociation|clean no-t]]

This theory seeks to explain why, when no one is watching, previously hypnotized people may later carry out [[posthypnotic suggestion|posthypnotic suggestions]]

![[posthypnotic suggestion|clean no-t]]

It also may explain why people hypnotized for pain relief may show activity in regions like the [[somatosensory cortex]], which receive sensory information, but not in areas that normally process pain-related information

###### Other factors

Hypnotists merely *focus people's attention* on certain images or behaviors
[[selective attention]] may be a factor. Brain scans show that hypnosis increases activity in the [[frontal lobes|frontal lobe]] attention systems, so it doesn't block sensory input itself, but may redirect attention to other [[stimulus|stimuli]]

Highly hypnotizable are especially suggestible and imaginative, in other words *gullible*
Their [[1.4b Brain Structure|brain]]s also display altered activity when under hypnosis in regions associated with pain, such as the [[Prefrontal cortex]]
