There are multiple ways to assess the effectiveness of [[psychotherapy]]:
- client perceptions
- clinician perceptions
- behavior changes

###### Client Perceptions
According to client perceptions, psychotherapy is very effective
An early survey of 2900 people found almost 90% who were "fairly well satisfied".
Among those who recalled feeling "fair" or "very poor", 90% were now feeling "very good", "good", or at least "so so"

But there some reasons for skepticism of client perceptions:
- **People often enter therapy in crisis**: If, with the normal flow of events, a crisis passes, people may attribute their improvement to therapy. [[5.4b Depressive Disorders|depressed]] people often get better no matter what they do.
- **Clients believe that treatment will be effective**: The [[placebo effect]], through their positive expectations, may heal them
- **Clients generally speak kindly of their therapists**: Even if the problems remains, clients typically try to find something positive to say about their treatment. 
- **Clients want to believe the therapy was worth the effort**: *Effort Justification*: Investing time and money into something motivates one to find something positive about it

###### Clinician Perceptions
Clinicians also have very positive perceptions of therapy.
Case studies have shown the treatment to be very successful. 

However, clients enter psychotherapy focused on their unhappiness and leave it focused on their well-being. 
Therapists don't hear from clients who experience only temporary relief and seek out new therapists.
Therapists are also vulnerable to [[0.2 Cognitive Biases|cognitive biases]], like any person.
[[confirmation bias]] may lead them to unconsciously seek evidence that confirms their beliefs and ignore contradictory evidence. 
*Illusory correlations* can lead them to perceive associations that do not exist

##### Outcome Research
To more objectively measure the effectiveness of psychotherapy, psychologists have turned to controlled research, with control groups. 

A summary of 24 studies of psychotherapy found that two-thirds of those receiving psychotherapy for hallucinations or [[delusions]] improved markedly. 
However, similar improvement occurred among those who were untreated, such as those on treatment waiting lists.
But this study had its flaws. It had a small sample. 

Modern studies can use *randomized clinical trials*, where researchers randomly assign people on a waiting list to therapy or to no therapy. Then they evaluate everyone and compare outcomes, using tests and assessments done by others who don't know whether therapy was given. 

A [[meta-analysis]] of 475 studies showed that the average client who receives therapy ends up better off than 80% of untreated individuals on waiting lists. 
![[Screenshot_20250125_225737.png|800]]

Future studies have replicated this principle: People not undergoing therapy often improve, but those undergoing therapy are more likely to improve - and to improve more quickly and with less risk of relapse
(One problem: Studies that find a positive effect from therapy are more likely to get published than those that find no therapy benefit)

After therapy people exhibit:
- improved insight
- improved emotional insight
- a more patient, outgoing personality

Some people with depression and [[5.4a Anxiety Disorders|anxiety]] may experience sudden symptom reductions between their treatment sessions. Those *sudden gains* bode well for long-term improvement

#### Which psychotherapies work best?

One general finding from studies is that the more specific the problem, the better psychotherapy might solve it
Those who experience [[Panic Disorder|panic]] or [[specific phobia|specific phobias]], those who are unassertive, or those with frustrations about sexual problems are more likely to improve. 
Those with less-focused problems, like depression and anxiety, usually benefit in the short-term, but relapse later.

Some forms of therapy are effective at treating certain problems:
- **[[5.5b Cognitive Therapies|cognitive]] and [[Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy]]** - primarily anxiety, [[posttraumatic stress disorder]], [[insomnia]] & [[5.4b Depressive Disorders|depression]]
- [[5.5b Behavior Therapies|Behavioral Conditioning Therapy]] - behavior problems such as bed-wetting, specific phobias, [[obsessive-compulsive disorder|compulsions]], marital difficulties, and sexual dysfunctions
- [[5.5a Psychodynamic Therapy|Psychodynamic Therapy]] - depression and anxiety. Some analyses suggest that psychodynamic therapy and CBT are equally effective in reducing depression
- [[person-centered therapy|nondirective (person-centered) counseling]] - mild to moderate depression

### Pseudotherapies
The tendencies for many disordered states of mind to return to better health naturally, combined with the placebo effect, allows pseudotherapies to become popular. 

Choosing what therapies count as psychotherapy has created a 'civil war' in psychology
To what extent should science guide both clinical practice and insurers' willingness to pay for psychotherapy

One one side are research psychologists using scientific methods to extend the list of well-defined and validated therapies for various disorders, who ardently decry those who "give more weight to their personal experience that to science"
On the other, are some nonscientific 'therapists' who view their practice as art, claiming that people are to complex and that psychotherapy is too intuitive to describe in a manual or test in an experiment.

Between these two factions are science-oriented clinicians calling for [[evidence-based practice]], which has been endorsed by the [[APA]]

![[evidence-based practice|clean no-t]]

Increasingly, insurer and government support for mental health services requires evidence-based practice

### How does Psychotherapy help?

- **Hope** - People seeking therapy typically feel anxious, depressed, self-disapproving and incapable of change. Psychotherapy offers an expectation that, with commitment, things can and will get better. This belief may improve morale, create feelings of self-efficacy & diminish symptoms
- **A new perspective** - psychotherapy can offer people a plausible explanation of their symptoms. With a believable fresh perspective, they may approach life with a new attitude, open to making changes in their behaviors and self-perception
- **An empathic, trusting & caring relationship** - Therapists are empathic. A strong [[therapeutic alliance]], the emotional bond between a therapist and client, can foster strong psychological health

![[therapeutic alliance|clean no-t]]

These elements explain why *paraprofessionals* (briefly trained caregivers) can assist many troubled people so effectively.
Healers everywhere, whether psychologists or shamans:
- listened to understand and empathize
- reassure
- advise
- console
- interpret
- explain
- are people who clients can disclose their suffering to

This is why people who feel supported by close relationships have been less likely to seek therapy

### Cultural Differences
Differences in culture can create a mismatch

When a therapist from an [[individualist]] culture interacts with a client from a [[collectivist]] culture, there may be difficulties
These clients may have trouble relating to therapists who ask them to think only of their own well-being

Matching with counselors with shared cultural values leads to greater perceptions of empathy from the counselor

There are other differences that may lead to mismatches. 
For example, highly religious people may prefer and benefit from religiously similar therapists. 

Cultural differences may also help to explain some groups' reluctance to use mental health services. 
Some from "cultures of honor" may feel that seeking mental health care is an admission of weakness
Because of this, refugees, despite frequently experiencing [[5.4a Trauma-and Stressor-Related Disorders|trauma]] & discrimination tend to avoid mental health services
