We use [[problem-focused coping]] when we believe stress is a problem to be solved - when we feel a sense of control over a situation
We use [[emotion-focused coping]] when we believe we cannot change a situation

![[problem-focused coping|clean no-t]]
![[emotion-focused coping|clean no-t]]

A perceived lack of [[personal control]] leads to:
- rise in stress hormones
- rise in blood pressure
- lowered immune response
- after repeated unavoidable aversive events, [[learned helplessness]] can develop

![[personal control|clean no-t]]
![[learned helplessness|clean no-t]]

Poverty reduces control, one of the many factors linking economic status and longevity

In general, increasing control can have a significant impact on mental and by extension physical health:
- allowing prisoners to move chairs and to control room lights and TV
- having workers participate in decision making
- allowing people to personalize their workspace

People who perceive an  [[internal locus of control]] achieve more, and have better health outcomes than those with an [[external locus of control]]

#### Outlook

Our coping with stress is influenced by our outlook 
<mark class="ls">Pessimists</mark> expect things to go badly
They attribute poor performance to a basic lack of ability, or to situations beyond their control
<mark class="ls">Optimists</mark> expect more control, coping ability, and better health

Optimists tend to get better grades, and it relates to well-being and stress
Optimism runs in families, if one identical twin is optimistic, the other typically will be too
