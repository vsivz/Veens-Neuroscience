The speaker, like most people, viewed stress negatively. 
The speaker read a study, where she saw a correlation between belief that stress is harmful, and experiencing harmful effects of stress.
She interpreted this as a causation, which changed her mind. 
   
   
   
2. Describe the study that influenced the speaker’s view on stress. What were its findings regarding stress and the belief about its effects on health?
The study saw a very significant correlation between belief that stress is harmful, and experiencing harmful effects of stress. 
People who didn't see stress as harmful seemed more resilient, even under substantial stress.
The speaker presented this correlation as a causation. This ignores other possible explanations, like people who experience more harmful physiological stress responses may have likely formed an association, consciously or subconsciously, that stress is harmful. 
   
   
   
3. How does rethinking your stress response change your body’s physiological reaction, according to the studies mentioned?
According to the studies, participants who reinterpreted their stress responses as helpful experienced relaxed blood vessels instead of constricted ones.
This generally improved cardiovascular health. 
   
   
   
4. What role does oxytocin play in the stress response, and how does it affect social behavior?
Oxytocin plays a far more complex role in the stress response than the speaker implies. While she states that it is a stress hormone as a fact, in general, Oxytocin is not classified as a stress hormone. It gets released in the stress response to act as a buffer to stress. It is also anti-inflammatory. Oxytocin is also known to facilitate social behavior. 
   
   
   
5. What does the research suggest about the effects of helping others on our stress levels and mortality rate?
The research strongly suggests that helping others is beneficial to reducing our stress levels. This is completely independent of the so-called benefits of stress that she was talking about earlier. Social bonding facilitates the release of Oxytocin, which is a buffer to stress. Stress does release Oxytocin, which will facilitate social bonding, demonstrating a form of stress self-regulation. This explains the finding that social bonding prevented stressful events from increasing mortality rate. Social bonding facilitates Oxytocin release, which crucially reduces stress. By reducing stress, you reduce the mortality rate from stressors. 



6. According to the speaker, how can changing your perspective on stress impact your health and longevity?
The speaker claims that changing one's perspective about stress can have positive impacts on health and longevity. 
By viewing the physiological responses as adaptive and beneficial, individuals can reduce the negative emotional impact of stress. 
   
   
   
7. Discuss the implications of viewing stress responses as beneficial. How might this perspective change your approach to stressful situations in your own life?
In my opinion, this Ted-Talk was very misleading. While appraising stressors as beneficial can bring benefits, the speaker leaves out the critical piece of info that the biggest health problems caused by stress are caused by chronic stress. Chronic stress, regardless of how we appraise it, has been shown to be very negative for our health and well-being. By framing stress as positive, the speaker encouraged her audience not to view chronic stress as a problem to fix, which can be very dangerous. However, there is some useful info to be gained about the fact that appraising single events as non-stressful can bring with it benefits. This can change my approach to stressful situations by appraising those stress responses as beneficial and not detrimental.
