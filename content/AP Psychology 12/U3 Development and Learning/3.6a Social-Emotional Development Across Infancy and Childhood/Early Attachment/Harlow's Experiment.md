For many years, psychologists believed that infants became attached to those who satisfied their need for nourishment. But an accidental finding showed us the truth.

In the 1950s, psychologists Harry and Margaret Harlow bred monkeys for studies. 
To equalize experiences, they <mark class="vi">separated the infant monkeys from their mothers</mark> shortly after birth, and raised them in individual cages, each of which included a <mark class="ii">cheesecloth baby blanket</mark>
Then, they saw that when the soft blankets were taken away, the monkeys <mark class="ii">became distressed</mark>

Then the Harlows realized this intense attachment to the blanket contradicted the previous consensus that attachment arises from nourishment

So they created two artificial mothers to directly compare a food source to contact comfort:
<mark class="ls">Wire Mother</mark>: a <mark class="vi">bare wire</mark> cylinder with a wooden head and an attached <mark class="vi">feeding bottle</mark>
<mark class="ls">Cloth Mother</mark>: a cylinder with <mark class="vi">no bottle</mark>, but covered in <mark class="vi">soft</mark> form rubber and wrapped in terry cloth

When raised with both artificial mothers, the monkeys <mark class="vi">overwhelmingly preferred the cloth mother</mark>
Anxious monkey babies would cling to their cloth mothers, soothed by this <mark class="kv">contact comfort</mark>
While <mark class="ii">exploring</mark> their environment, they used her as a <mark class="kv">secure base</mark>

Researchers soon learned that other qualities, <mark class="ii">rocking, warmth, and feeding</mark>, made the cloth mother even more appealing
