Sexual Development begins in <mark class="lc">[[Prenatal Development]]</mark>
<mark class="na">6 weeks</mark> after conception, you looked just like any other embryo
Then as your genes kicked in, your <mark class="ii">biological sexual characteristics</mark>, determined by your <mark class="na">23rd</mark> pair of chromosomes (the two sex chromosomes), became more apparent
Your mother's contribution was an <mark class="kv">X chromosome</mark>, from your father, you received the only <mark class="vi">non-unisex</mark> chromosome- another X chromosome, making you <mark class="vi">female</mark> or a <mark class="kv">Y chromosome</mark> making you male. Occasionally there are other sexual development variations.

About <mark class="na">7 weeks</mark> after conception, a single gene on the Y chromosomes triggers the <mark class="ii">testes to develop</mark> and to produce <mark class="kv">testosterone</mark>, the main <mark class="kv">androgen</mark> (male hormone) that promotes male sex organ development. 
Females <mark class="ii">also have testosterone, but have less of it</mark>
The main female sex hormones are the <mark class="kv">estrogens</mark>, such as estradiol

Later, during the <mark class="na">4th and 5th prenatal months</mark>, sex hormones influence the wiring of the prenatal brain. 
Different pattern for male and female develop under the influence of the male's greater testosterone and the female's estrogens
If females are prenatally exposed to unusually high levels of male hormones, they tend to grow up with more male-typical interests
