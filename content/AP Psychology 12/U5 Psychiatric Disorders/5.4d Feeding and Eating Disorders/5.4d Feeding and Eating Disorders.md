>[!psych] feeding and eating disorder
> a group of [[psychological disorder|disorders]] characterized by altered consumption of food that impairs health or psychological functioning. (Feeding disorders typically occur in infants and young children, whereas eating disorders affect people who self-feed)

>[!qdsm] Quote from the DSM-5-TR
>Anorexia Nervosa > Differential Diagnosis > Bulimia Nervosa
> **"** Individuals with bulimia nervosa exhibit recurrent episodes of binge eating, engage in inappropriate behavior to avoid weight gain (e.g., self-induced vomiting), and are overly concerned with body shape and weight. However, unlike individuals with anorexia nervosa, binge-eating/purging type, individuals with bulimia nervosa maintain body weight at or above a minimally normal level. **"**


>[!dsm] Info from the DSM-5-TR
>If an individual who binge-eats/purges was originally diagnosed with Anorexia Nervosa, binge-eating/purging type, but no longer meets the requirements (weight normalizes) they may be diagnosed with Bulimia Nervosa when all criteria have been met for three months

The families of people with anorexia tend to be competitive, high-achieving, and protective

Eating disorders share commonalities with [[5.4a Anxiety Disorders|anxiety disorders]]
Those with eating disorders:
- have low body satisfaction
- set perfectionist standards
- [[rumination|ruminate]] about falling short of expectations and how others perceive them

There is a genetic component. Identical twins share these disorders more often than do fraternal twins, indicating 50-60% heritability for anorexia

But this has a far more significant cultural and [[gender]] component

The rise in eating disorders in the last half of the twentieth century coincided with a dramatic decline in Western women's body image
Eating disorders result from today's weight-obsessed culture. 

Most diagnosed with an eating disorder do improve. In one 22-year study, 2 in 3 women with anorexia or bulimia had recovered.
