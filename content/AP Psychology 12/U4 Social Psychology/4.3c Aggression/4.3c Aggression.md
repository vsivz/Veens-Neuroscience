Aggressive behavior emerges from the interaction of biology and experience

###### Biological Factors
**Genetic Factors**
[[genes]] influence aggression
animals have been bred for aggressiveness - sometimes for sport, sometimes for research
The effects of genes also appears in human [[twin studies]]
If one [[identical twins|identical twin]] admits to "having a violent temper", the other twin will often independently admit the same

Researchers continue to search for genetic markers in those who commit violent acts. 
One of these markers is the [[Y Chromosome]] itself
Another is the [[MAO-A]] gene, which regulates an enzyme responsible for breaking down [[neurotransmitter|neurotransmitters]]
People with low *MAOA* expression tend to behave more aggressively when provoked

**Neurological Factors**
There is no one spot in the [[1.4b Brain Structure|brain]] that controls aggression, which is a complex behavior that occurs in particular contexts
But animal and human brains have neural systems that, given provocation, will inhibit or facilitate aggression

The [[amygdala]] is a region that, after being stimulated by an electrode, facilitates aggression
If the impulse-controlling [[frontal lobes]] are damaged, inactive, disconnected or not yet fully mature, aggression may be more likely

**Biochemical Factors**
Our [[1.2 Overview of the Nervous System|Nervous Systems]] operate electrochemically
The [[hormones|hormone]] [[testosterone]], for example, influences the neural systems that control aggression
Many animals become very aggressive, or gentle, after their testosterone levels are increased or decreased respectively
Humans are less sensitive than animals, but are still influenced
Drugs that sharply reduce testosterone levels also subdue men's aggressive tendencies

Another drug, [[alcohol]], *unleashes* aggressive responses to frustration. 
Alcohol is a dis-inhibitor, and under its influence, people are more likely to interpret ambiguous acts as aggressive.
